## 1. `london-leiden-056-how-to-dismiss-what-you-called`

### Existing draft

- Exact original record: [`ancientContentSeed.json`, line 1482](../functions/ancientContentSeed.json#L1482)
- Title: **How to Dismiss What You Called**
- Tradition: Graeco-Roman Egypt
- Category: BanishingAndRelease
- Status: draft
- Existing content: an informational explanation of the fumigant and spoken dismissal at the end of a vessel-divination procedure. Its practical activity only asks the reader to write a closing sentence.

### Proposed rewrite — Closing the Reflecting Bowl

- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Primary category: `protection-cleansing-warding`
- Secondary category: `divination`
- Summary: Learn a smoke-free closing sequence that ends a self-directed water-scrying session, separates observation from interpretation, and returns the bowl to ordinary use.
- Introduction: The *Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden* gives a specific dismissal after vessel divination. Column III, lines 28–30 pairs a fumigant with words sending the summoned presences away. This is a Roman-period Egyptian procedure, not ancient Wicca. The modern adaptation retains its clearest practical lesson—a divination has a deliberate ending—while removing smoke, coercion, and claims that an entity appeared.
- Tools: A dark bowl; plain water; a towel; notebook and pen; ordinary room lighting.
- Correspondences: Documented technique—vessel divination followed by dismissal; documented material context—fumigation, omitted here; modern associations—water as the reflective surface and ordinary light as grounding.

#### The lesson

1. Place the empty bowl on a stable, dry table. Set the towel beneath it, switch on enough room light to move safely, and decide how long the session will last. Write one question about your own choices. Avoid questions claiming certainty about another person, health, law, money, or an emergency.
2. Add a shallow layer of plain water. Sit where you can look toward the surface without bending your neck or staring into glare. State that the bowl is being used as a focus for reflection. This purpose statement is a modern Wiccan boundary, not wording from the papyrus.
3. Look softly at the surface for several minutes. Notice shapes, memories, feelings, or associations without treating them as predictions. Record short words only if doing so does not break your focus. Stop early if you become distressed or feel compelled to keep checking.
4. When the chosen time ends, stop seeking new impressions. Say, “This inquiry is complete. All attention called here is released in peace.” These are original modern words; they neither translate nor reproduce the historical dismissal.
5. Turn fully toward the room. Name three ordinary things you can see and feel both feet on the floor. Write one sentence beginning “I observed” and another beginning “I interpret.” This keeps the experience separate from the meaning you later give it.
6. Pour the water into a sink, rinse and dry the bowl, and return it to ordinary storage. Do not drink the water or save it as medicine. The cleanup makes the ending physical as well as spoken.
7. Close the notebook and do an everyday task. Revisit the entry later only if it remains useful. A completed closing means the session is not reopened repeatedly to chase certainty.

- Optional incantation: “Water made still, reflection now done; I close this seeing and return as one.” Original modern wording.
- Practice: Complete one ten-minute bowl reflection and use steps 4–7 to close it.
- Reflection: Which part of the closing most clearly marked the return from interpretation to ordinary attention?
- Safety note: Keep water away from electrical equipment and wipe spills immediately. This practice is reflective, not predictive or medical. Do not use smoke or a historical fumigant, and stop if the exercise increases distress or compulsive checking.
- Adaptation notes: Keeps a vessel, inquiry, spoken dismissal, and defined ending. Omits fumigation and supernatural certainty. Adds time limits, grounding, observation-versus-interpretation notes, cleanup, and consent boundaries.
- Citation: F. Ll. Griffith and Herbert Thompson, *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, vol. I (London: H. Grevel & Co., 1904), P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. III, lines 28–30. [Public-domain edition transcription](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html); [British Museum manuscript record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10070-2).

## 2. `london-leiden-064-threatening-the-tools`

### Existing draft

- Exact original record: [`ancientContentSeed.json`, line 1697](../functions/ancientContentSeed.json#L1697)
- Title: **Threatening the Tools**
- Tradition: Graeco-Roman Egypt
- Category: DivinationAndClarity
- Status: draft
- Existing content: a historical discussion of coercive speech directed toward a lamp wick. Its activity asks the reader to describe an everyday object rather than conduct a practical working.

### Proposed rewrite — The Listening Lamp

- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Primary category: `candle-magic`
- Secondary category: `divination`
- Summary: Prepare a safe light as a bounded focus for reflective inquiry, replacing the source’s threat toward the wick with respectful modern wording.
- Introduction: The London-Leiden handbook treats a lamp and its wick as active parts of a divinatory operation and addresses the lamp directly. The historical speech includes pressure and threatened denial of oil. This modern Wiccan adaptation retains preparation, direct address, focused light, and formal closure while rejecting coercion. It uses a battery candle by default and does not claim that the light is conscious or gives supernatural answers.
- Tools: Battery candle; heat-safe tray if an adult independently chooses a wax candle; notebook and pen; timer.
- Correspondences: Documented ritual focus—lamp and wick; documented action—direct ritual address; modern associations—light as attention and the timer as a firm boundary.

#### The lesson

1. Choose a question about your own next action. Write it in a form that permits uncertainty, such as “What should I consider before deciding?” Set a timer for ten minutes. Clear the surface and place the battery candle where it cannot fall.
2. Hold the unlit candle or rest your hands beside it. Describe its actual function aloud: it provides a point of light and a place to rest your attention. The historical text addresses the lamp as a participant; here, direct address is used to organize the practitioner’s attention, not to claim the object is alive.
3. Switch on the candle and say, “Steady light, help me attend; my choices remain my own.” This is original modern wording. It replaces the source’s coercive demand and makes the ethical change explicit.
4. Look beside the light rather than directly into it. Take five unforced breaths. For each breath, name one fact you know about the question. When facts run out, label what follows as a hope, fear, assumption, or possibility.
5. Write three short headings: “Known,” “Uncertain,” and “Next step.” Add at least one item under each. If an image or association arises while looking near the light, record it under “Uncertain” until ordinary evidence supports it.
6. When the timer sounds, stop. Say, “The light is released from this work; the question returns to my judgment.” Switch off the battery candle. If a responsible adult chose a wax candle, snuff it completely and verify that the wick and holder are cold before leaving.
7. Put the tool away and carry out only the ordinary next step you recorded. Do not repeat the session that day to force a preferred answer. The completed action, rather than a promised omen, closes the working.

- Optional incantation: “Steady flame or gentle glow, show where fact and question flow; when this measured work is through, judgment rests with what I do.” Original modern wording.
- Practice: Use the light once to sort one question into known facts, uncertainties, and an achievable next step.
- Reflection: Did addressing the light change your attention without making the object responsible for your decision?
- Safety note: Prefer a battery candle. If using flame, keep it on a fire-resistant surface, away from fabrics, pets, children, and drafts; never leave it unattended. Do not stare into bright light, use oils on the candle, or treat impressions as professional advice.
- Adaptation notes: Retains prepared light, direct address, inquiry, and closure. Replaces coercion with respectful language, removes historical ingredients and divine claims, and adds a timer, evidence labels, and flameless access.
- Citation: Griffith and Thompson, *Demotic Magical Papyrus*, vol. I (1904), P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, the lamp address spanning recto col. VI–VII; the existing seed locator “col. VI, 11–21” requires line-level editorial correction before approval. [Edition transcription](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html); [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10070-2).

## 3. `london-leiden-149-the-sixteen-thread-band`

### Existing draft

- Exact original record: [`ancientContentSeed.json`, line 3989](../functions/ancientContentSeed.json#L3989)
- Title: **The Sixteen-Thread Band**
- Tradition: Graeco-Roman Egypt
- Category: DivinationAndClarity
- Type: potion
- Status: draft
- Existing content: an informational description of a band using sixteen colored threads, bird blood, a scarab, and attachment to a child medium. Its activity is only a journal prompt. The seed incorrectly cites column IV; the passage is at column III, lines 33–35.

### Proposed rewrite — Four-Color Boundary Cord

- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Primary category: `cord-knot-magic`
- Secondary category: `divination`
- Summary: Make a removable sixteen-thread cord that marks the beginning and ending of a self-directed reflective practice without animal material or bodily binding.
- Introduction: Column III of the London-Leiden papyrus specifies sixteen linen threads—four white, four green, four blue, and four red—made into one band for a child medium. It also requires animal blood and a scarab. This substantial safety adaptation retains only the documented count, colors, textile construction, and relationship to divination. Braiding, personal intention, storage, and the ethical limits are original modern additions.
- Tools: Four threads each of white, green, blue, and red cotton or linen; scissors; removable clip or tape; storage card or pouch.
- Correspondences: Documented—linen, sixteen threads, four of each named color, one band; undocumented—separate color meanings, so none are assigned; modern function—a boundary cue for reflective practice.

#### The lesson

1. Choose one self-directed practice that benefits from a firm beginning and end, such as tarot journaling, quiet observation, or recording a dream. Write its purpose in one sentence and choose a stopping time before touching the thread.
2. Cut sixteen equal lengths: four white, four green, four blue, and four red. Count them twice. The source gives this exact structure but does not explain individual color meanings, so do not present the colors as ancient elemental or intention correspondences.
3. Align the ends and secure them to a table with a removable clip or tape. Divide the bundle into three workable sections while keeping the colors mixed. Never anchor the cord to your body, furniture that may tip, or anything near flame.
4. Begin a loose braid. At the first crossing say the practical purpose you wrote. At later crossings, name the boundaries of the activity: how long it will last, what question belongs to you, and what kinds of claims you will avoid.
5. When the braid is long enough to sit beside your tool, tie one loose overhand knot at each end. These knots are modern construction choices. Do not make a bracelet, collar, restraint, or charm placed on another person.
6. Lay the cord beside the notebook or divination tool. Speak the optional incantation, complete the chosen practice once, and stop at the predetermined time. Treat the cord as a reminder of the boundary rather than a guarantee of clarity.
7. Coil the cord onto its storage card. Record whether seeing and storing it helped you finish. If it frays, cut it into short unusable pieces before disposal so it cannot entangle a child, pet, or wildlife.

- Optional incantation: “Four colors gathered, one purpose in view; I begin with care and finish when due.” Original modern wording.
- Practice: Braid one sixteen-thread cord, place it beside one brief reflective activity, and store it when the activity ends.
- Reflection: Did the cord help define the practice without becoming a promise that the result must be true?
- Safety note: Keep loose fibers and scissors away from children and pets. Never tighten cord around a neck, limb, finger, another person, or an animal. Keep it away from flame and omit all blood, insects, and animal remains.
- Adaptation notes: Retains thread number, colors, combination into one band, and a divinatory setting. Removes the child medium, blood, scarab, bodily attachment, and promised speed. Adds braiding, boundaries, storage, and original wording.
- Citation: Griffith and Thompson, *Demotic Magical Papyrus*, vol. I (1904), P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. III, lines 33–35. [Edition transcription showing the transition to col. IV after line 35](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html); [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10070-2).

## 4. `london-leiden-061-the-child-medium`

### Existing draft

- Exact original record: [`ancientContentSeed.json`, line 1616](../functions/ancientContentSeed.json#L1616)
- Title: **The Child Medium**
- Tradition: Graeco-Roman Egypt
- Category: DivinationAndClarity
- Status: draft
- Existing content: a historical account of testing and preparing a boy as a medium. It expressly says the procedure must not be attempted and gives only a reading exercise.

### Proposed rewrite — The Clean Table of Inquiry

- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Primary category: `altars-sacred-space`
- Secondary category: `divination`
- Summary: Prepare, use, and fully clear a stable ritual table for one adult, self-directed inquiry.
- Introduction: A related operation in the same London-Leiden handbook places a clean four-footed table in a clean room, covers it, arranges four bricks and a censer, maintains silence, and seeks an answer. The larger divinatory material also uses a child medium. This modern Wiccan adaptation rejects child involvement and spirit testing. It retains cleaning, four-point arrangement, covering, silence, one inquiry, and restoration of the space.
- Tools: Stable table or tray; clean cloth; four flat coasters or tiles; notebook and pen; optional cards, lots, or another familiar reflective tool.
- Correspondences: Documented—clean room, four-footed olive-wood table, covering, four bricks, censer, silence; modern substitutions—stable coasters, notebook, smokeless and flameless setup.

#### The lesson

1. Select a stable table in a quiet room and write one question about your own conduct or priorities. Set a fifteen-minute limit. Remove unrelated objects, wipe the surface, and leave clear walking space around it.
2. Put one flat coaster or tile near each corner. The historical procedure uses four bricks; unstacked flat pieces preserve a visible four-point arrangement without creating an unstable structure. Lay the cloth across the center so no edge becomes a trip or fire hazard.
3. Place the notebook and your familiar reflective tool at the center. Do not add a censer, charcoal, incense, animal fat, oil, or flame. No child or other person serves as a medium. This is an adult’s self-directed reflective practice.
4. Stand at the table and say, “This clean place holds one honest question; it does not command my answer.” This is original modern wording. Sit in silence for five unforced breaths, noticing the table’s four corners and the ordinary room beyond it.
5. Conduct one brief reading, draw one lot, or journal freely for five minutes using a method you already understand. Record what occurred as an impression. Beneath it, write at least one alternate interpretation and one fact that could confirm or challenge it.
6. When the timer ends, stop even if the result feels incomplete. Thank any deity you personally invited without claiming that an ancient Egyptian presence was summoned. Put away the reflective tool and close the notebook.
7. Remove the cloth and four pieces, wipe the surface if needed, and return the table to everyday use. Restoring the table is part of the working: sacred space is created for a purpose and then deliberately released.

- Optional incantation: “Clear is the table, bounded the time; reflection may speak, while choice remains mine.” Original modern wording.
- Practice: Prepare the four-point table, complete one bounded inquiry, and restore the surface immediately afterward.
- Reflection: Which physical act—cleaning, arranging, covering, or clearing—most changed your attention?
- Safety note: Keep the table stable, exits open, and cloth edges controlled. This version is smoke-free and flameless. Use divination for reflection, not emergency, medical, legal, or financial decisions, and never recruit a child or nonconsenting person as a medium.
- Adaptation notes: Retains ordered cleaning, covered table, four-point layout, silence, inquiry, and closing. Substitutes flat pieces for bricks and removes the censer, formulas, spirit claims, prolonged silence, and child-medium practice.
- Citation: Griffith and Thompson, *Demotic Magical Papyrus*, vol. I (1904), P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. IV, lines 1–8, read alongside the child-medium procedures at col. III, lines 11–20 and col. X, lines 9–22. [Edition transcription](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html); [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10070-2).

## 5. `maqlu-022-water-closes-what-fire-opened`

### Existing draft

- Exact original record: [`ancientContentSeed.json`, line 566](../functions/ancientContentSeed.json#L566)
- Title: **Water Closes What Fire Opened**
- Tradition: Mesopotamian (Akkadian / Standard Babylonian)
- Category: BanishingAndRelease
- Status: draft
- Existing content: an informational explanation of the *Maqlū* transition from burning to sprinkling and repeated handwashing at dawn. Its practical activity is a brief mindful handwash.

### Proposed rewrite — Dawn Water Closure

- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Ancient Mesopotamian anti-witchcraft ritual practice
- Primary category: `protection-cleansing-warding`
- Secondary category: `elemental-magic`
- Summary: Use ordinary handwashing at dawn or the start of the day to close a difficult period of reflection without reproducing the source’s burning rites.
- Introduction: The *Maqlū* ritual tablet moves from destructive fire symbolism to quenching, sprinkling, repeated washing, and dawn formulas. This is ancient Mesopotamian ritual, not Wicca. The adaptation isolates the attested movement toward water and morning, removes burning and accusations against other people, and turns handwashing into a modern personal closure practice with no medical or supernatural claim.
- Tools: Sink with clean running water; ordinary soap; clean towel; small piece of paper and pen; recycling or waste bin.
- Correspondences: Documented sequence—fire followed by quenching, sprinkling, handwashing, and dawn; documented element—water; modern function—ending one’s own reflective work and choosing an ordinary next action.

#### The lesson

1. At dawn or near the beginning of your day, write one burden you are ready to stop rehearsing. Describe your own fear, habit, or unfinished reflection; do not name another person as an enemy or accuse anyone of witchcraft.
2. Beneath it, write one action within your control for today. Keep the paper dry and place it beside, never inside, the sink. This written step is a modern addition that gives the closing a practical destination.
3. Turn on comfortably warm water. Wet your hands and apply ordinary soap. As you wash the palms, say, “What has ended need not be carried.” As you wash the backs of the hands, say, “What remains can meet the morning.” These words are original and do not translate a *Maqlū* formula.
4. Clean between the fingers and around the nails for the ordinary recommended handwashing duration. Notice the temperature and movement of water without claiming it absorbs illness, danger, curses, or another person’s influence.
5. Rinse completely. Watch the soap leave, then turn off the tap to avoid waste. Dry your hands thoroughly. The physical completion—water stopped, hands dried, towel returned—is the boundary of the working.
6. Read the action you wrote. Tear off and recycle or discard the sentence naming the burden; keep the action sentence if it will help. Do not burn either piece. The historical fire sequence is context only and is deliberately excluded.
7. Open a curtain or step into ordinary daylight if safe, then complete the chosen action. Repeat the practice on another day only for a newly defined reflection, not as a compulsive attempt to guarantee protection.

- Optional incantation: “Morning has opened; water has passed. My hands meet the work that is mine at last.” Original modern wording.
- Practice: Complete the seven-step handwashing closure once and follow it with the written ordinary action.
- Reflection: Did the transition from water to a concrete action create a clearer ending than reflection alone?
- Safety note: Use a safe water temperature, dry spills, conserve water, and protect irritated or broken skin. This is symbolic hygiene, not treatment or guaranteed cleansing. No fire, smoke, historical substances, or accusations against another person are used.
- Adaptation notes: Retains the documented progression toward water, washing, and dawn. Omits burning, hostile accusation, ritual claims, and reconstructed formulas. Adds self-directed wording, paper disposal, water conservation, and an achievable next action.
- Citation: *Maqlū* Ritual Tablet, obverse lines 148′–161′, handwashing and dawn sequence, ORACC/CMAwR text Q002713. [ORACC text record](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/cmawro/Q002713).
