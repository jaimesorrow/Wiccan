## Candidate 1 — Closing a Scrying Session

- Status: `draft — review required`
- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Contemporary application: A modern witchcraft closing practice for a self-directed scrying session. This is an adaptation, not an ancient Wiccan rite.
- Primary category: `divination`
- Lesson type: `divination`
- Source: *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. III, lines 28–30.
- Edition: F. Ll. Griffith and Herbert Thompson, 1904, vol. I.
- Source evidence: the passage directs dismissal after vessel divination and records a brief dispatch formula.
- Rights finding: the 1904 edition is public-domain in the United States; Griffith died in 1934 and Thompson in 1944, placing their 1904 work outside life-plus-70 terms. Use original explanatory prose and no museum images. Final product counsel should confirm every distribution jurisdiction.
- Rights/reference links: [UPenn Online Books author and edition record](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/who/Griffith%2C%20F.%20Ll.%20%28Francis%20Llewellyn%29%2C%201862-1934), [Google Books public-domain notice](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Demotic_Magical_Papyrus_of_London_an.html?id=nGrIvQEACAAJ).
- Manuscript dating: early third century CE; catalogue confirmation: [British Museum EA10070](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10070-2) and [Getty discussion of the early-third-century bilingual papyrus](https://www.getty.edu/news/multilingualism-along-the-nile/).

### Summary

Learn to end a self-directed scrying session with a clear dismissal, grounding, and a written distinction between observation and interpretation.

### Introduction

This lesson adapts one small but essential feature of a Roman-period Egyptian vessel-divination procedure: the session has an explicit ending. The source belongs to a late-antique Egyptian ritual tradition, not Wicca. Its closing instruction can nevertheless inform a modern witchcraft practice in which divination is bounded, reflective, and deliberately concluded.

### Tools

- Your usual self-directed scrying tool, such as a dark bowl of water or a mirror
- A notebook and pen
- A cloth or cover for the tool

The notebook and cover are modern additions. The ancient passage records dismissal, not this storage routine.

### Numbered method

1. Before beginning your own scrying method, decide on one question about your choices or experience. Avoid questions that claim certainty about another person's thoughts or the future.
2. Complete the scrying practice you already use. Record impressions as images, associations, or feelings rather than facts or predictions.
3. When you are ready to close, stop seeking new impressions. In your own words, thank any deities or spirits you deliberately addressed. If you addressed none, acknowledge that the period of attention is complete.
4. State a brief dismissal. A historically inspired paraphrase is: “Go in peace; this inquiry is ended.” This is modern wording, not a translation of the ancient formula.
5. Cover or put away the scrying tool. Turn on ordinary room lighting, take a breath, and notice your immediate surroundings. These grounding actions are modern safety adaptations.
6. Write one sentence beginning, “I observed…,” and one beginning, “I interpret this as….” Keeping observation separate from interpretation helps prevent a fleeting image from being treated as certainty.
7. Close the notebook and return to an ordinary task. Revisit the entry later only if reflection remains useful.

### Formula handling

No direct source translation is included in the lesson. The brief line in the 1904 edition is retained in the editorial evidence file; the lesson uses an explicitly modern paraphrase.

### Optional incantation

“In peace, this seeing closes; I return to the place where I stand.” This is original modern wording, not a historical formula.

### Reflection

How did a deliberate ending change the way you understood the impressions from the session?

### Practice

Close one self-directed divination session with steps 3–7 and record the difference between observation and interpretation.

### Safety note

Use divination for reflection, not for medical, legal, financial, or emergency decisions. Stop if the practice increases distress or compulsive checking. Do not use it to claim knowledge of another person's private thoughts or consent.

### Adaptation notes

- Self-directed adult practice replaces the source's child medium.
- Animal materials, burning substances, spirit compulsion, and coercive elements elsewhere in the larger procedure are excluded.
- The modern paraphrase does not reproduce the ancient formula.
- Grounding, notebook, cover, consent, and interpretive limits are modern additions.

### Correspondences

- Documented technique: vessel divination followed by dismissal
- Modern additions: covered tool, ordinary room light, written reflection

## Candidate 2 — A Lamp and Dream Inquiry

- Status: `draft — review required`
- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Contemporary application: A modern witchcraft dream-reflection practice adapted from a lamp inquiry. It is not an ancient Wiccan rite or a reconstruction.
- Primary category: `divination`
- Secondary category: `candle-magic`
- Lesson type: `divination`
- Source: *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. V, lines 3–24.
- Edition, dating, and rights basis: as recorded for Candidate 1.
- Source evidence: the passage prepares a clean dark place and a lamp, recites an invocation, looks toward the lamp, lies down in silence, and expects an answer by dream.

### Summary

Learn a bounded dream-reflection practice that preserves the source sequence of preparation, focused light, silence, sleep, and later interpretation.

### Introduction

The London-Leiden papyrus preserves a lamp inquiry in which ritual preparation, focused attention, silence, and sleep form one sequence. This lesson keeps that sequence as its historical anchor while changing the hazardous and culturally specific parts. The modern application treats a dream as material for reflection, not a guaranteed message or prediction.

### Tools

- A flameless lamp or battery candle
- A stable surface away from bedding
- A notebook and pen

The flameless light and notebook are modern safety adaptations. The source specifies an oil lamp, wick, brick, incense, writing on the wick, recitation, and a rush mat; those elements are omitted or changed here.

### Numbered method

1. Choose a question about your own conduct, priorities, or creative work. Phrase it so that more than one interpretation remains possible.
2. Place the flameless light on a stable surface. Dim the room enough for quiet focus while keeping a safe path to the door. Do not work in total darkness.
3. Write the question in your notebook. Below it, write: “I will notice what the dream suggests; I will decide what it means after waking.” This is modern wording.
4. Sit comfortably and look beside the light rather than staring into it. Take seven unforced breaths. The count of seven echoes repetition in the passage but is a modern substitution for its repeated invocation.
5. Read your question once. No ancient formula is used: the source's invocation belongs to its Egyptian religious setting, and removing it is a substantial adaptation.
6. Turn off the light, go to sleep normally, and keep the notebook within easy reach. Do not use smoke, incense, oils, eye preparations, or an open flame near bedding.
7. On waking, record only what you remember before interpreting it. Mark gaps plainly instead of filling them in.
8. Later that day, identify one possible interpretation and one ordinary action you could take. If no dream is remembered, record that result and close the practice without repeating it compulsively.

### Formula handling

The source has an invocation, but reproducing or loosely rewriting its divine names would imply a reconstruction this lesson does not make. The optional words below are an original modern incantation and do not translate the source.

### Optional incantation

“As I rest, let images arise; at waking, I will weigh them with care.”

### Timing

The source places the inquiry at night and links it to sleep. The seven breaths are a labeled modern adaptation; they are not the source's recitation.

### Reflection

What changed when you treated the dream as an image to interpret rather than an answer you had to obey?

### Practice

Complete the eight-step inquiry once and record both the remembered material and the limits of your interpretation.

### Safety note

Use a flameless light near sleeping areas. Do not burn oil, candles, or incense in bed or while drowsy. Do not ingest or apply historical ingredients. Dreams are not reliable evidence about another person or a substitute for professional advice.

### Adaptation notes

- The oil lamp, incense, marked wick, brick, ritual purity rule, rush mat, and extended invocation are omitted.
- A flameless light, safe room lighting, notebook, consent boundary, and interpretive framing are modern additions.
- Seven breaths retain a visible connection to the passage's repeated action while being clearly identified as a substitution.
- The expected supernatural answer is reframed as reflective dream material.

### Correspondences

- Documented timing: night, followed by sleep
- Documented ritual focus: a prepared lamp
- Modern substitution: flameless light

## Candidate 3 — A Four-Color Cord for Focus

- Status: `draft — review required`
- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Contemporary application: A modern witchcraft braided focus charm. It is a substantial safety adaptation, not an ancient Wiccan rite or a reconstruction.
- Primary category: `cord-knot-magic`
- Secondary category: none
- Lesson type: `cord`
- Source: *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. III, lines 33–35.
- Edition, dating, and rights basis: as recorded for Candidate 1.
- Source evidence: the passage specifies sixteen linen threads—four each of white, green, blue, and red—combined into one band and worn as part of a vessel-divination operation.
- Passage link: [Griffith and Thompson translation, col. III 33–35](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html).

### Summary

Learn to braid the source's four-color, sixteen-thread structure into a removable reminder for a self-directed divination practice.

### Introduction

The papyrus describes a colored linen band intended to help a child medium's vessel divination. It also calls for animal blood and a drowned scarab. This lesson preserves only the documented thread count, colors, and act of making one band. It removes the animal materials, child involvement, bodily binding, and promise of magical speed. The result is a modern witchcraft focus charm: a visible cue to begin, bound, and end one's own reflective work.

### Tools & materials

- Sixteen loose cotton or linen threads: four white, four green, four blue, and four red
- Scissors
- A card, pouch, or box for storage

Cotton and the storage container are modern options. No color meaning is assigned because the passage does not explain the colors.

### Numbered method

1. Choose one self-directed activity that needs a firm boundary, such as journaling, tarot, or quiet reflection. State its practical purpose in one sentence.
2. Cut the sixteen threads to equal, comfortable lengths. Count four of each documented color. Keep them loose on a clear surface; do not tie them around any body part.
3. Gather all sixteen threads and divide them into three manageable sections without inventing a meaning for the color placement.
4. Braid slowly. With each crossing, repeat the purpose in plain language. Braiding is the modern working; the source only says to make the threads into one band.
5. Secure each end with a loose overhand knot. Place the finished band beside the tool for the chosen activity.
6. Speak the optional modern incantation once, complete the activity, and stop at the time you set beforehand.
7. At the end, coil the band without tightening it around yourself or anyone else. Put it in its container and record whether the physical boundary helped your attention.

### Optional incantation

“Four colors gathered, one purpose in view; I begin with care and finish when due.” This is original modern wording.

### Reflection

Did making and storing the band help you give the practice a clearer beginning and ending?

### Practice

Braid one sixteen-thread band, use it beside one brief reflective activity, and then store it.

### Safety note

Keep loose fibers away from children and pets. Never tighten a cord around the neck, wrist, fingers, circulation, or another person. Stop using frayed cords and keep them away from flame. Do not use blood, insects, or other animal remains.

### Adaptation notes

- Retains the source's sixteen threads, four documented colors, and formation of one band.
- Omits animal blood, the scarab, the child medium, bodily attachment, and the guaranteed-speed claim.
- Braiding, the focus intention, storage, time boundary, and incantation are original modern additions.
- The colors are not given invented elemental, planetary, or intention correspondences.

### Correspondences

- Documented materials: linen; white, green, blue, and red threads
- Documented construction: four threads of each color made into one band
- Modern function: a non-worn focus and boundary cue

## Candidate 4 — Preparing a Clean Table for Inquiry

- Status: `draft — review required`
- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical practice
- Contemporary application: A modern witchcraft sacred-space setup for self-directed inquiry. It is not an ancient Wiccan rite or a reconstruction.
- Primary category: `altars-sacred-space`
- Secondary category: `divination`
- Lesson type: `altar`
- Source: *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, recto col. IV, lines 1–8.
- Edition, dating, and rights basis: as recorded for Candidate 1.
- Source evidence: the procedure sets a clean four-footed olive-wood table in a clean room, covers it, places four bricks and a censer, recites a formula, keeps silence, and lies down for an inquiry.
- Passage link: [Griffith and Thompson translation, col. IV 1–8](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html).

### Summary

Learn to prepare, use, and close a clean ritual table for a bounded divination question while replacing the source's smoke, animal ingredients, and spirit formula.

### Introduction

In this late-antique Egyptian operation, a specially prepared table and room create the setting for divine inquiry. Modern Wiccan and witchcraft practice often gives similar practical importance to a deliberately prepared altar or sacred workspace. This adaptation keeps the source's sequence of cleaning, placing, covering, silence, and inquiry. It changes the religious address and removes the brazier mixture, supernatural certainty, and overnight demand.

### Tools & materials

- A stable four-legged table or tray on a stable table
- A clean cloth large enough to cover the surface
- Four stable coasters or flat tiles
- A notebook, pen, and your usual self-directed divination tool

The tray, coasters, notebook, and divination tool are modern substitutions. They do not reproduce the source's olive wood, stacked bricks, censer, or formula.

### Numbered method

1. Write one question about your own choices. Phrase it for reflection rather than a guaranteed answer.
2. Clear and wipe the table. Check that it does not wobble and that paths around it remain open.
3. Place one coaster or tile near each corner. Do not stack them. Lay the clean cloth across the surface, keeping its edges away from the floor and walkways.
4. Set the notebook and divination tool at the center. Use no charcoal, incense, animal fat, myrrh mixture, or open flame.
5. Sit in silence for a short time you choose. Speak the optional modern incantation, then conduct one self-directed reading or observation using the method you already know.
6. Record the result as an impression and at least one alternative interpretation. Do not treat it as a command or evidence about another person's private thoughts.
7. Close the working by putting away the divination tool, removing the cloth, and returning the table to ordinary use. Wash or store the cloth as appropriate.

### Optional incantation

“I clear this place and steady my sight; may reflection be honest and choices remain mine.” This is original modern wording.

### Reflection

Which part of preparing and clearing the table most affected your attention?

### Practice

Prepare the table, complete one brief self-directed inquiry, and restore the surface afterward.

### Safety note

Keep the table stable and exits clear. This version is flameless and smokeless; do not add a censer, charcoal, incense, animal ingredients, or unattended flame. Use divination for reflection, not medical, legal, financial, or emergency decisions.

### Adaptation notes

- Retains a clean room, clean table, covering, four supporting objects, silence, and an inquiry sequence.
- Uses unstacked coasters or tiles to avoid unstable bricks.
- Omits the censer, charcoal, animal fat, myrrh mixture, Greek formula, spirit appearance, and overnight silence.
- The question framing, notebook, divination limits, cleanup, and incantation are original modern additions.

### Correspondences

- Documented space: a clean room
- Documented furniture: a clean, four-footed olive-wood table with a covering
- Documented arrangement: four bricks and a censer
- Modern substitutions: stable surface, four unstacked coasters or tiles, no smoke or flame

## Candidate 5 — An Eye Symbol as a Written Reminder

- Status: `draft — scope and safety review required`
- Tradition: Wiccan / Modern Witchcraft
- Source tradition: Roman-period Egyptian magical-medical practice
- Contemporary application: A modern witchcraft written charm used only as a reflective reminder. The source's medical aim is not retained, and this candidate must remain blocked unless an editor approves that changed scope.
- Primary category: `protection-cleansing-warding`
- Secondary category: none
- Lesson type: `tool-light`
- Source: *The Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden*, P. BM EA 10070 / P. Leiden I 383, verso col. XXI, line 7 (following the col. XX medical formula and topical preparation).
- Edition, dating, and rights basis: as recorded for Candidate 1.
- Source evidence: the passage directs writing on new papyrus, making it into an amulet worn on the body, and includes an eye-with-rays drawing. It appears beside an ophthalmia treatment.
- Passage link: [Griffith and Thompson translation, verso col. XX–XXI](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html).

### Summary

Learn to make a removable paper eye symbol that reminds you to pause and assess a space; it offers no medical treatment or promised protection.

### Introduction

The source combines a spoken medical charm and a topical preparation with a written papyrus amulet bearing words and an eye symbol. Reproducing that treatment would be unsafe and making a health claim would be misleading. This modern witchcraft adaptation isolates the documented act of writing and carrying a symbol, then gives it a limited new purpose: prompting ordinary awareness before entering or leaving a space. Because that changes the historical objective, the lesson remains an editorial review candidate rather than an approved reconstruction.

### Tools & materials

- A small piece of plain paper
- A nontoxic pen or pencil
- A small envelope or card sleeve

Paper replaces papyrus. The sleeve is a modern addition that avoids applying ink or preparations to skin.

### Numbered method

1. Choose one ordinary awareness check, such as noticing exits, checking that a candle is out, or deciding whether a room feels calm enough to stay in.
2. On the paper, draw a simple eye with short rays. Create your own design rather than copying a museum image or claiming an exact facsimile.
3. Beneath it, write one concrete prompt: “Pause, look, choose.” This is original modern wording, not the translated historical formula.
4. Place the paper in the sleeve. Do not anoint it, attach it directly to skin, or add oil, salt, nasturtium, pigments, or historical medicines.
5. Before entering or leaving the selected space, hold the sleeve and complete the awareness check. Take any ordinary safety action the check reveals.
6. Speak the optional modern incantation once. Put the sleeve in a pocket or bag without claiming that it creates an invisible barrier.
7. After one week, record whether the prompt helped. Recycle or store the paper; replace it only if the practice remains useful.

### Optional incantation

“May I notice clearly, pause without fear, and choose my next step with care.” This is original modern wording.

### Reflection

Did the symbol lead to a concrete act of awareness, or did it become a promise you expected the object to keep for you?

### Practice

Use the sleeved symbol for one week as a prompt for the same ordinary awareness check.

### Safety note

This charm does not prevent harm, diagnose or treat eye problems, or replace medical care, locks, alarms, supervision, or emergency action. Do not use the historical ointment, place any preparation in or near the eyes, apply ink to skin, or give the charm to someone without consent. Seek qualified care for eye symptoms.

### Adaptation notes

- Retains only the documented actions of writing on a new surface, making a portable amulet, and using an eye symbol.
- Omits the medical claim, spoken formula, oil, salt, nasturtium, topical application, and direct bodily attachment.
- Changes the aim from treating ophthalmia to prompting ordinary situational awareness; this material change requires explicit scope approval.
- The design, wording, sleeve, one-week review, disposal options, and incantation are original modern additions.

### Correspondences

- Documented object: written papyrus amulet
- Documented sign: an eye with rays
- Historical context: adjacent ophthalmia treatment, which this lesson does not reproduce
- Modern function: a portable awareness prompt
